## 0. TL;DR for the art director

- **Spine:** Kalidasa's *Meghaduta* (*The Cloud Messenger*, c. 4th–5th c. CE). An exiled demigod begs the first cloud of the rains to carry a message north to his wife, and gives it a route across half of India. It is a 1,600-year-old travel scroll with a built-in payoff: the message gets delivered. Arthur W. Ryder's 1912 English verse is public domain and quotable as written.
- **Look:** Pahari and Rajput monsoon miniatures. Ink-dark or slate skies, gold zig-zag lightning, a diagonal line of white cranes, peacocks crying on trees, lovers on a white balcony, a floral or gold border. Add Indian popular print as a second voice: matchbox labels and Ravi Varma oleographs, with flat saturated inks, slight misregistration, halftone grain and condensed hand-lettered caps.
- **Sound:** Raag Miyan ki Malhar for the storm (komal ga with a slow andolan, both nishads around dha, the R–P Malhar leap). Raag Megh for the serene, overcast pentatonic opening and close. Under it a tanpura drone, rain, tin roofs, frogs, crickets and pakhawaj-like thunder.
- **Life:** the first drop on hot dust (*petrichor*, bottled as *mitti attar*), paper boats, chai and pakoras, a power cut and a lantern, rain on tin, peacocks, the onset front marching north from Kerala on 1 June to cover the whole country by 8 July (IMD normal), the cloudburst (100 mm in one hour), the clearing.
- **My pick:** Concept A, *The Cloud Messenger*. Rain words set the intensity of each chapter, and the IMD onset map is woven into the route. Reasons are in §5.

## 1. Literature

### 1.1 Kalidasa, *Meghaduta* (*The Cloud Messenger*)

**Story.** A Yaksha, an attendant of Kubera the god of wealth, is cursed with a year's exile for neglecting his duty. He spends it on Ramagiri ("Rama's peak", traditionally identified with Ramtek near Nagpur), in central India, while his young bride is far north in Alaka, the Yaksha city on Mount Kailasa. After eight months the first monsoon cloud butts against his peak "as an elephant attacks a bank of earth in play". The rainy season is when separated lovers suffer most, and he fears his wife will not survive it, so he offers the cloud fresh blossoms and asks it to be his messenger. He then describes the whole route north and the sights along it, then the heavenly city, his house, his grieving wife, and finally the message itself.

**Structure.** Ryder notes 115 four-line stanzas in the "slow-stepper" metre (*mandakranta*). The poem comes in two halves:
- *Purvamegha*, "Former Cloud" (63 stanzas in Ryder): the request and the **journey**.
- *Uttaramegha*, "Latter Cloud" (52 stanzas): **Alaka**, the house, the wife, and the **message**.

Ryder also summarises the plan: "from this peak [near Nagpur] he flies a little west of north to the Nerbudda River, and the city of Ujjain; thence pretty straight north to the upper Ganges and the Himalaya." He suggests Kalidasa took the idea from the aerial-car journey in the *Ramayana*.

**The route as chapters**, using Ryder's English place names (an ideal scroll):
1. Rama's peak, the exile's mountain (I–XIV).
2. Mala plateau, where farmers' wives look up because the furrow depends on the cloud (XVI).
3. Mango Peak, whose forest fires the cloud once put out (XVII).
4. The Reva (Narmada) foaming on the Vindhya rocks, "like painted streaks upon an elephant's dingy hide" (XIX).
5. Dasharna country and Vidisha on Reed River. Here the cloud must "subdue thy thunders to a tone / Of murmurous gentleness" (XXIII–XXIV).
6. Ujjain, Kalidasa's beloved city, with the Mahakala shrine at dusk. The cloud wears "twilight's rose-red glory" as Shiva's robe for his dance (XXVII–XXXVII).
7. Deep River, Holy Peak, Skin River, the Ten Cities, the Hallowed Land (the old battlefield).
8. The Ganges falling "like a flight of stairs from heaven let down" (L), then the Himalaya, its musk, deodars and yaks.
9. The Swan-gate pass, then Kailasa, then **Alaka**, where "streams of rain like strings of close-inwoven pearls" hang over her face (LXIII).
10. Latter Cloud: the house, the wife counting the days, the message, the secret token, the blessing.

**Verses worth using (Ryder 1912, public domain).** Source: Project Gutenberg eBook #16659, *Translations of Shakuntala and Other Works*, https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/16659

> On Rama's shady peak where hermits roam, / Mid streams by Sita's bathing sanctified, / An erring Yaksha made his hapless home, / Doomed by his master humbly to abide, / And spend a long, long year of absence from his bride. (I)

> Some months were gone; the lonely lover's pain / Had loosed his golden bracelet day by day / Ere he beheld the harbinger of rain, / A cloud that charged the peak in mimic fray, / As an elephant attacks a bank of earth in play. (II)

> Even happy hearts thrill strangely to the cloud (III, line 4). *This line works as a tagline.*

> How all unfitly might his message mate / With a cloud, mere fire and water, smoke and wind (V). *"Fire and water, smoke and wind" is a ready-made shader brief.*

> While favouring breezes waft thee gently forth, / … the cranes who know thy worth / Will meet thee in the sky on joyful wings (IX). *This is the crane row from the miniatures.*

> When mushrooms sprout to greet thy fertile weeks, / The swans who long for the Himalayan lake / Will be thy comrades to Kailasa's peaks (XI)

> Learn first, O cloud, the road that thou must go, / Then hear my message ere thou speed away; / Before thee mountains rise and rivers flow: / When thou art weary, on the mountains stay, / And when exhausted, drink the rivers' driven spray. (XIII). *This is the perfect "how to scroll" instruction screen.*

> Scenting the sweet, sweet smell of forest earth, / The deer will trace thy misty track that ends the dearth. (XXI). *Petrichor, 1,600 years before the word existed.*

> Where women steal to rendezvous by night / Through darkness that a needle might divide, / Show them the road with lightning-flashes bright (XXXVII)

> Fly then where Ganges o'er the king of mountains / Falls like a flight of stairs from heaven let down (L)

> Himalaya's breeze blows gently from the north, / … I embrace it lovingly; it came so far, / Perhaps it touched thee first, my life's unchanging star! (Latter, XLIV)

> Our fates move up and down upon a circling wheel. (Latter, XLVI)

> Then wander free / In rainy loveliness, and ne'er abide / One moment's separation from thy lightning bride. (Latter LII, the final line)

**Why it works as a scroll journey.**
- **It is already a route.** The poem reads as a list of waypoints south to north, so scroll progress literally equals geographic progress. One axis covers both time and space.
- **It has a reason to keep scrolling.** There is a message to deliver, and it is held back until the end.
- **The reader becomes the cloud.** The poem is addressed to "thou, O cloud", so second person is native to the text. The site can address the visitor as the cloud.
- **Weather is built into the poem.** The cloud rests on mountains, drinks rivers, is told to hush its thunder over Vidisha, wears dusk at Ujjain, quenches fires in the Himalaya and pours "strings of pearls" at Alaka. Each beat is a weather state.
- **It has a famous secret token.** The wife once woke from a dream crying "Rogue! Rogue! I saw thee with another girl in dream" (Latter XLVIII). It is intimate and funny, and gives a good final reveal.
- **The ending is seasonal.** The curse ends "when Vishnu rises from his serpent bed" (Latter XLVII), when the rains end. The site can end with autumn moonlight, the clearing.

### 1.2 *Ritusamhara* (*The Gathering of the Seasons*), canto 2, "The Rains" (*Varsha*)
- Six cantos, one per Indian season: Grishma (summer), Varsha (rains), Sharad, Hemanta, Shishira and Vasanta. It opens in summer and closes in spring, 144 stanzas in all. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E1%B9%9Atusa%E1%B9%83h%C4%81ra and Kalidasa Academy, https://www.kalidasacademy.com/about-kalidasa/
- Canto 2 has 28 verses. Its famous opening image is the **monsoon as a king arriving in procession**: water-laden clouds are his rutting elephants, lightning his banners, thunder his drums (sanskritdocuments.org gloss: https://sanskritdocuments.org/sites/giirvaani/giirvaani/rs/rs_2.htm). Other images: chataka birds begging the clouds for drops, the kadamba and ketaki blossoming "as if the forest has laughed", and peacocks dancing.
- **Public-domain English:** Ryder's book includes a short verse paraphrase, *The Seasons* (same Gutenberg #16659). Its rains stanza:

> The rain advances like a king / In awful majesty; / Hear, dearest, how his thunders ring / Like royal drums, and see / His lightning-banners wave; a cloud / For elephant he rides, / And finds his welcome from the crowd / Of lovers and of brides.

> The clouds, a mighty army, march / With drumlike thundering / And stretch upon the rainbow's arch / The lightning's flashing string; / The cruel arrows of the rain / Smite them who love, apart / From whom they love, with stinging pain, / And pierce them to the heart.

  *Use:* the "rain as a royal procession" image is a strong idea for the storm-arrival chapter, with drums, banners and elephants made of cloud.

### 1.3 Tagore
- **Gitanjali** (English, translated by the author, 1912/1913) is public domain. Project Gutenberg #7164: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/7164. The rain poems:
  - **18:** "Clouds heap upon clouds and it darkens. Ah, love, why dost thou let me wait outside at the door all alone? … I know not how I am to pass these long, rainy hours. I keep gazing on the far-away gloom of the sky, and my heart wanders wailing with the restless wind."
  - **22:** "In the deep shadows of the rainy July, with secret steps, thou walkest, silent as night, eluding all watchers. Today the morning has closed its eyes … The woodlands have hushed their songs, and doors are all shut at every house. Thou art the solitary wayfarer in this deserted street."
  - **23:** "Art thou abroad on this stormy night on thy journey of love, my friend? The sky groans like one in despair. … By what dim shore of the ink-black river, by what far edge of the frowning forest … art thou threading thy course to come to me, my friend?" *This one mirrors the Meghaduta: a traveller through the storm toward a waiting lover.*
  - **45:** "In the rainy gloom of July nights on the thundering chariot of clouds he comes, comes, ever comes." *"He comes, comes, ever comes" works as a recurring refrain or loading line.*
- **Bengali rain songs:** Tagore's song collection (*Gitabitan*) groups seasonal songs under *Prakriti* (nature), with a large *Barsha* (rains) group. Well-known examples include "Esho shyamalo sundoro" and "Mon mor megher shongi". Treat these titles as general knowledge: I did not open a source for them. His Bengali lyrics are public domain (he died in 1941), but any modern English translation may not be, so use Tagore's own 1912–13 English.

### 1.6 The scent of first rain
- **Petrichor** was coined by Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Grenfell Thomas (CSIRO) in *Nature*, 1964, from *petra* (stone) and *ichor* (the blood of the gods). They admitted they were not the first to capture it. Kannauj perfumers were already making it as **mitti attar**, "earth's perfume". Source: The Atlantic, https://www.theatlantic.com/international/archive/2015/04/making-perfume-from-the-rain/391011. The earthy molecule is geosmin, made by soil bacteria (India Today, https://www.indiatoday.in/lifestyle/fashion/story/what-goes-into-petrichor-perfume-baked-earth-and-bacteria-geosmin-for-mitti-ki-khushboo-in-a-bottle-2597139-2024-09-10).
- **How mitti attar is made (deg-bhapka).** Kiln-baked clay discs or broken kulhads go into a copper **deg** with water. The lid is sealed with mud or multani mitti and heated over a wood or cow-dung fire. Vapour travels through a bamboo pipe (*chonga*) into a long-necked copper **bhapka** of sandalwood oil, sitting in a trough of cooling water. This is repeated daily for 10+ days, and up to ~60 days for the best grades. The finished attar was traditionally stored in camel-leather flasks. Sources: Atlas Obscura, https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/smell-of-rain-kannauj-perfume-mitti-attar-india; The National, https://www.thenationalnews.com/weekend/2023/08/04/monsoon-rain-perfume-attar/; Elle India, https://elle.in/beauty/this-indian-factory-has-the-100-year-old-secret-of-the-scent-of-rain-9460067. The government Fragrance & Flavour Development Centre says only about 10% of Kannauj's ~400 perfumeries make mitti (Hindustan Times, 2016).
- **Use:** an "attar" interlude, a little copper still where scroll turns the fire and the oil darkens drop by drop. It is a smell rendered visually: amber and clay tones and slow rising steam. Kalidasa's own line fits it: "Scenting the sweet, sweet smell of forest earth" (XXI).

## 2. Visual art

### 2.1 Conventions of monsoon painting (Barahmasa, Ragamala, Rasikapriya)
**Barahmasa** means "twelve months". Pahari painters (Kangra, Guler) illustrated Keshavdas's *Kavipriya* (1601), chapter 10, one painting per month, each built around a couple, the nayaka and nayika. In the rain months the heroine argues that no one should travel. LACMA's translation of the verses for *Bhadon* and *Sawan* (https://collections.lacma.org/object/27856):
- **Sawan:** "the rivers … meet sea … The creepers have clung to trees. The lightning meets the clouds and shine all around. The peacocks … make happy sounds announcing the meeting of earth and sky. Thus, all the lovers are meeting their beloveds."
- **Bhadon:** "The dark clouds have gathered all around and are thundering loudly … cicadas are chirping continuously … There is no longer any difference between day and night … One's own home is like nectar and outside is like poison."

**Ragamala** means "garland of ragas": each raga is painted as a scene. **Megha / Megh Mallar Raga** is consistently shown as Krishna or a prince **dancing in the rain**, holding a vina, between women musicians on drum (mridangam) and cymbals (manjira). Two peacocks face each other below, and the rain falls in long streaks from dark clouds. Sources: VMFA, https://www.vmfa.museum/artworks/page-from-a-ragamala-series-megha-raga-72418; Harvard, https://harvardartmuseums.org/collections/object/310659; LACMA, https://collections.lacma.org/object/56958. The LACMA 1605–06 folio carries a Sanskrit verse from the *Sangita Ratnakara*: "Of blue splendor, attached to the roar of the raincloud; of tender body and lovely form … is said to be Megha Mallar Raga."

The British Museum's Jaipur Megha folio: "thunderclouds **ringed in gold** with **orange snake-like streaks of lightning**" (https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1999-1202-0-2-31).

**The recurring visual vocabulary:**
- **Sky band at the top:** a band of black, slate or indigo, often with cloud scallops outlined in white or gold. At the Met, the Malwa *Setmalar Ragini* has "bands of heavy clouds and snakelike bolts of lightning across the upper register" (https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/37883).
- **Lightning** drawn as a sinuous gold, red or orange line, like a snake. It reads as ornament, not light.
- **White cranes or egrets** in a diagonal or curved row cutting across the dark cloud. In Worcester Art Museum's label, "cranes overhead … symbolic of love to be fulfilled" (https://worcester.emuseum.com/objects/190/). A Kangra painter quoted in Verve noticed "there is one among them flying to its own tune."
- **Peacocks** on trees or rooftops, necks stretched, crying. A pair facing each other means love.
- **Rain** as long fine parallel white streaks, or simply implied. In the Met's words: "the depiction of rain is not illusory, but rather simply suggestive" (https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/ragas-and-ragamalas).
- **Architecture:** white pavilions, balconies, red doors, a bed in a dark chamber below.
- **Flat colour fields**, especially Malwa and Rajasthani, with strong red borders.
- **Kangra** adds atmospheric depth: an oval vignette, a floral spandrel border and gold rules.
- **Other rain themes:** Krishna lifting Govardhan, or sheltering the gopis under a blanket or cloak (Met, https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/37968). Women on swings at **Teej**, the Sawan festival (Cleveland 2018.197).

### 2.3 Raja Ravi Varma and early Indian popular print
- Ravi Varma (1848–1906) joined Indian myth to European academic oil technique. In 1894 he set up a press to mass-produce **oleographs and chromolithographs**, which put gods and heroines on every wall (Britannica, https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ravi-Varma). He painted Kalidasa's *Shakuntala* many times, including *Shakuntala writing a love letter on a lotus leaf*, a public-domain image at the Ashmolean.
- I found no Ravi Varma work on the Meghaduta. The strongest **Meghaduta painting series** is by **Ramgopal Vijaivargiya**, a Bengal-school artist from the Swadeshi era (The Heritage Lab, https://www.theheritagelab.in/meghdoot-kalidas-ramgopal-vijaivargiya/). Check copyright before using any of it; treat it as a mood reference only.
- **Use:** the oleograph surface (soft academic modelling, varnish sheen, rich crimson and turquoise, a printed border) suits one "calendar art" plate, such as the wife at the window in Alaka. Matchbox labels, made from the 1930s mostly in **Sivakasi**, mix mythology, wildlife and nationalist slogans in flat, misregistered colour, grainy halftone and multi-script lettering (Latin, Tamil, Devanagari). Sources: V&A IS.13-2012, https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1258670/matchbox-label-unknown; Tasveer Ghar's Gautam Hemmady collection on Google Arts & Culture, https://artsandculture.google.com/story/new-india-in-a-matchbox-tasveer-ghar/nAVxk6SORHLICg; It's Nice That on designers treating "colour misalignments, grainy textures, and halftone patterns" as "the language of the medium", https://www.itsnicethat.com/features/the-view-from-mumbai-matchbook-graphic-design-130426.

## 3. Music

### 3.1 The lore
- **Tansen** (16th century, one of Akbar's nine jewels) is said to have brought rain with Malhar. In one telling, his singing of Raag **Deepak** lit every lamp and set him burning, and his daughter **Saraswati** (in some versions his wife) put out the fire by singing **Megh Malhar**. Sources: Britannica, https://www.britannica.com/biography/Tansen; Darbar, https://player.darbar.org/videos/ustad-amjad-ali-khan-rain-ragas-megh-miyan-ki-malhar-sarod-double-tabla-music-of-india
- Wikipedia lists others credited with bringing rain through some form of Malhar: Baiju Bawra, Bilas Khan, Meera Bai and more (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malhar). **Frame this as legend, not fact**; raga.one says so explicitly.
- **Miyan ki Malhar** is Tansen's version. It is also called *Gayand Malhar*, because "both nishads swing around the dhaivat like a (gayand) elephant swinging his head" (Wikipedia Malhar). Kalidasa's clouds are elephants too, so the two traditions rhyme.
- **Seasonal mood:** Sruti says Miyan ki Malhar is "explicitly euphoric at the onset of the rainy season (June–July)", while Megh Malhar represents "the sombre, and even awesome, facet of the advanced monsoon (July–August)", prescribed around midnight (https://www.sruti.com/articles/spotlight/raga-megh-malhar).

### 3.2 Notes and phrases
**Raag Miyan ki Malhar** belongs to the Kafi thaat (S R g m P D n S). Its vadi (most important note) is Pa and samvadi (second) is Sa. It is sung in the rainy season, at night or any time.
- Komal **ga** appears only in descent, "intoned with a slow and repeated oscillation" (andolan) (https://autrimncpa.wordpress.com/miyan-ki-malhar/).
- **Both Ni** follow each other with a touch of Dha or Sa between them: the **n D N S** turn. Ragajunglism: "special vakra treatment of the twin ni swaras is considered essential to generating the charged energies of bursting clouds" (https://ragajunglism.org/ragas/miyan-ki-malhar).
- **R P** is the Malhar-family leap, **m(m)R ; m(R)P**.
- It closes on the Kanada-ang phrase **(m) g m R S**.
- Pakad per musicmaster.in: *N̤ S m R P, D n N S, n P m P (g) m R S* (https://musicmaster.in/blog/raag-megh-malhar-monsoon-raga/).

**Raag Megh** (Megh Malhar in some usage) is pentatonic, **S R m P n** (komal ni). Equivalents: Carnatic Madhyamavati, the "Egyptian" pentatonic (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megh_(raga)). Vadi Sa, samvadi Pa. The mood is serene and grave. Pakad: *R R S, n̤ S, m R P, m R n̤ S*. Its glide R→P and repeated R give the feeling of overcast heaviness.

**Instruments and timbres:**
- **Tanpura** drone on Sa–Pa (or Sa–ma).
- **Sarod or sitar** for meend (glides). Classic recordings: Amjad Ali Khan's Megh and Miyan ki Malhar on Darbar; Budhaditya Mukherjee's Miyan ki Malhar during a thunderstorm.
- **Sarangi** or voice for andolan on komal ga.
- **Bansuri** for phuhaar-light passages.
- **Pakhawaj or tabla**: deep bols as thunder.
- **Jal tarang** (tuned water bowls), which literally sound like drops.
- **Santoor** tremolo as rain.
- **Swarmandal** shimmer.

### 3.3 Evoking it in synthesised WebAudio (no samples needed)
- **Tuning:** pick Sa at about 146.8 Hz (D3) or 138.6 Hz (C#3). Use just intonation:
  - Re 9/8
  - komal ga 6/5
  - ma 4/3
  - Pa 3/2
  - Dha 5/3
  - komal ni 9/5 (or 16/9)
  - Ni 15/8
- **Tanpura:** four plucked voices cycling Pa–Sa'–Sa'–Sa (low) every ~4 s. Each voice is a sawtooth plus noise through a resonant band-pass. The *jawari* buzz comes from slowly sweeping a second band-pass (about 1.5–3 kHz) across the decay, plus slight detune and sympathetic overtones. A long convolution reverb (generated impulse) adds space.
- **Melody voice:** a single oscillator (triangle mixed with filtered saw) with portamento.
  - **Meend** = `linearRampToValueAtTime` across 200–600 ms.
  - **Andolan** on komal ga = an LFO of ±20–35 cents at 0.6–1 Hz, used only in descent.
  - Signature cells to sequence: `m R P` · `n D N S'` · `S' n P` · `m P (g~) m R S`.
  - A tiny generative system can pick phrases from this grammar so it never loops identically.
- **Rain:** pink noise through high-pass filters, with grain density set by intensity. Individual drops are short sine "plinks" (1.5–4 kHz) with randomised pitch. **Tin roof:** short metallic resonances (band-passes at about 900 Hz, 2.1 kHz and 3.4 kHz) excited by noise bursts.
- **Thunder:** brown noise into a low-pass that opens and closes, with a delayed rumble tail. The distance cue is a 0.5–4 s delay after a lightning flash.
- **Pakhawaj thunder:** pitched sine kick (60→40 Hz) plus a slap of noise.
- **Frogs and crickets:** frogs are FM chirps (carrier ~300–600 Hz, modulator ~30–60 Hz) in pulse trains. Crickets are high sine pulses (~4.5 kHz) at about 15–20 per second, sped up as temperature or brightness rises.
- **Peacock:** a two-note rising "may-awe" call from a formant-filtered saw, 400→900 Hz.
- **Jal tarang drops:** sine plus a short pitch drop, tuned to the Megh pentatonic. Melody then literally falls as rain.
- **Mix law:** in the calm chapters use Megh (pentatonic, serene). As intensity rises, switch to Miyan ki Malhar (the twin-Ni turn and andolan become more frequent). At the clearing, resolve to a long Sa with a solo tanpura.
- **Accessibility:** sound starts on a user gesture, with a visible mute toggle.

## 4. Monsoon life details (what makes it feel lived)

**Before the rain (Grishma).**
- White sky, the *loo* (hot dry wind), cracked earth.
- Matkas (clay pots) sweating, khus curtains, terrace sleeping.
- Everyone glancing up. The chataka bird of poetry drinks only raindrops.
- *Ritusamhara* canto 1 is summer: "pracaṇḍasūryaḥ", the fierce sun.

**The first rain (pehli baarish).**
- The temperature drops all at once. Dust lifts in a gust before the drops arrive.
- The first fat drops make dark coins on hot concrete, which hiss and vanish.
- Then the smell: petrichor, *mitti ki khushboo*.
- Children run out. People step onto terraces and balconies.
- "The first drops are intercepted before they can embrace the earth, a collective gasp encircles the air" (India Currents, https://indiacurrents.com/rain-again/).

**Small rituals.**
- **Paper boats** (*kaagaz ki kashti*) made from old notebooks and raced in gutters.
- **Chai** with ginger, **pakoras** or bhajiyas, roasted **bhutta** (corn) with lime and salt, *jalebi*.
- Wet clothes that never dry, the smell of damp, mould in July.
- Black umbrellas: the classic men's black *chhata*.
- Rubber chappals that flick mud up your back. Plastic sheets over cycle-rickshaws.
- **Teej swings** in Sawan, and mehndi.

**The sounds.**
- Rain on **tin roofs** (a different drum on every house).
- **Frogs** start up within hours, and crickets at dusk.
- Dripping eaves and spouts (*parnaala*), gutters overflowing.
- **Peacocks crying** before and after rain. Peacocks and pea-hens are the classic omen.
- Termite swarms around the light with "gossamer golden wings". Jacobin cuckoos, the *chatak*, are said to arrive with the monsoon. Both from Indian Express, https://indianexpress.com/article/express-sunday-eye/mumbai-delhi-lifetime-of-monsoon-memories-10793371/

**Power cuts.**
- The bulb flickers and dies. Then a lantern or candle, everyone in one room, antakshari, stories, the ceiling fan slowing to a stop.
- The world shrinks to the sound of rain.

**The onset as a front.** IMD normal dates, 1961–2019 climatology, from IMD's research report *New Normal Dates of Onset/Progress and Withdrawal of Southwest Monsoon over India* (Pai et al., 2020), https://internal.imd.gov.in/press_release/20200515_pr_804.pdf:
- Andaman and Nicobar ~22 May. Central Bay of Bengal 26 May.
- **Kerala 1 June** (standard deviation ~7 days).
- NE India ~5 June. Goa and south Maharashtra 10 June. **Mumbai 11 June**.
- Most of West Bengal, Odisha and Telangana by 15 June.
- Gujarat south, Madhya Pradesh south by 20 June; the east by about the same time. **Delhi 27 June**.
- Most of the north by 30 June. **Entire country 8 July** (the old 1901–40 normal was 15 July).
- **Withdrawal** begins in west Rajasthan ~17 September and leaves most of the country by 15 October, when the northeast monsoon sets in over the south.

The real seasons vary: 2025 hit Kerala on 24 May and covered India by 29 June; 2026 hit Kerala on 4 June and covered India by 9 July (IMD season summaries).

IMD describes the advance as coming "usually in surges" along the **Northern Limit of Monsoon (NLM)** line (https://mausam.imd.gov.in/responsive/monsooninformation_onset.php). Animating the NLM as a living front line that pulses forward in surges is a strong, true visual. Nagpur (16 June) sits near Kalidasa's Ramagiri, so the Yaksha's peak gets its rain around mid-June. That date is a lovely bridge between the poem and the data.

**Cloudburst.** IMD defines it as ≥100 mm of rain in one hour at a station, usually over about 20–30 km². Sources: IMD FAQ, https://mausam.imd.gov.in/imd_latest/monsoonfaq.pdf; IITM Climate Lab, https://www.climate.rocksea.org/cloudbursts. One millimetre of rain is about 1 litre per m², so a cloudburst drops about 100 litres on every square metre in an hour. The Hindu adds that this can be about 10% of a city's annual rain in one hour. The mechanism is orographic lift into towering cumulonimbus, and it is commonest in the Himalaya, the NE and the Western Ghats. **Handle with care:** cloudbursts kill people every year. Treat the event as awe, not spectacle.

**The clearing.**
- Washed light, a rainbow (*Indradhanush*, "Indra's bow", which Kalidasa puts on an ant-hill in XV).
- Puddles reflecting the sky, steam rising off roads, leaves glossy.
- Snails and red velvet mites (*birbahuti*).
- Washed clothes finally on the line. Then autumn: Kalidasa's "nights that the full autumn moon illumines bright".

## 5. Three narrative concepts

### Concept A: "MEGHDOOT / The Cloud Messenger" ★ recommended
**Hook:** *You are the first cloud of the rains. An exile has given you a message. Carry it north.*

**Opening.** A short, quiet screen: "On Rama's shady peak…". Then the visitor is invited to **write (or pick) a line of message** for someone far away. The site keeps it locally and never uploads it. It becomes the Yaksha's message. At the end it is delivered, written in rain on a window in Alaka.

**Chapters** (scroll = flight north; each chapter is titled with a rain word, so intensity rises):
1. **Ramagiri, "बूँदाबाँदी Boonda-baandi".** Summer haze over a stylised Vindhya ridge. The first cloud bumps the peak like an elephant (XIV). The Yaksha offers blossoms. Ryder I–II, V ("fire and water, smoke and wind"). The petrichor moment: first dark drop-coins on red earth.
2. **The road is taught, "फुहार Phuhaar".** XIII ("Learn first, O cloud, the road"). A miniature-style **map scroll** unrolls. Kalidasa's route is drawn in gold over the IMD onset isochrones, which fill in as the visitor scrolls: Kerala 1 June → Nagpur ≈16 June → Delhi 27 June → all of India 8 July. Cranes form a row beside the visitor (IX).
3. **Reva and the forest, "रिमझिम Rimjhim".** Fine rain over the Narmada on black rocks (XIX), deer following the misty track (XXI), peacocks crying on jasmine hills (XXII). Village life fades in and out in small vignettes: paper boats, chai steam, frogs.
4. **Ujjain at dusk, "बौछार Bauchhaar".** Wind-slanted rain. Rooftops, palace balconies, peacock pets. The cloud wears "twilight's rose-red glory" for Shiva's dance at Mahakala (XXXVI). Night falls with lightning to light lovers' paths "through darkness that a needle might divide" (XXXVII). Here the matchbox and oleograph layer appears: lanterns, a power cut, an attar still.
5. **The Hallowed Land and the Ganges, "झड़ी Jhadi".** Rain that will not stop. The Ganges "like a flight of stairs from heaven let down" (L), Jamuna and Ganga mixing dark into white (LI).
6. **Himalaya and the Swan-gate, "मूसलाधार Mooslaadhaar → बादल फटना".** The full storm. Malhar at its height, deodars, quenched fire (LIII), thunder in caves as Shiva's drums (LVI). A short, respectful cloudburst moment: the screen fills, then silence.
7. **Alaka, "खुल गया Khul gaya" (the clearing).** The city with rain like strings of pearls (LXIII). The wife at the window counting days. Rain writes the visitor's own message on the glass. The secret token is revealed ("Rogue! Rogue!…"). Final line: "…ne'er abide / One moment's separation from thy lightning bride." Autumn moon, rainbow, then credits ("hapybeing").

- A **Kangra-Malwa miniature world** made live: a stacked top sky band, gold hairline lightning, crane rows, a scalloped cloud edge, flat terrain planes, a floral border frame that thickens and thins with intensity.
- Clouds are volumetric (shader: "fire and water, smoke and wind") but **outlined in gold** like painted thunderclouds.
- Interludes in matchbox and oleograph print for the "lived" vignettes.

### Concept B: "RIMJHIM SE MOOSLAADHAAR, a Dictionary of Rain"
**Hook:** *Hindustani has a word for every kind of rain. Scroll through all of them, one shower at a time.*

**Chapters** (each is one word; the whole page gets wetter):
1. **Garmi / Loo** (pre-rain). White heat, a chataka waiting. Text: Ritusamhara summer.
2. **Pehli boond / Boonda-baandi + Mitti.** First drops. An interactive **mitti attar still**: scroll stokes the fire, and an oil drop gathers the smell of earth. Bear and Thomas, 1964, versus Kannauj.
3. **Phuhaar.** A fine mist you "walk through": the cursor parts the spray. Bansuri.
4. **Rimjhim.** Steady rhythm on a tin roof. A drum you can play by tapping roof panels, each tuned to Raag Megh. Paper boats in a gutter.
5. **Bauchhaar / Jhadi.** Wind-driven slants, then rain that won't stop. Power cut, lantern, antakshari: a dark chapter lit only by the cursor-lantern. Tagore's Gitanjali 22.
6. **Mooslaadhaar / Badal phatna.** Pestle-thick streams. The page itself floods. IMD's 100 mm/hour is shown as a filling glass. Malhar at full intensity.
7. **Khul gaya / Indradhanush.** The clearing. Puddles reflect everything you scrolled past, and a peacock dances.

- Ravi Varma oleograph plates for human moments.

### Concept C: "1 JUNE → 8 JULY, the Walk of the Monsoon"
**Hook:** *Every summer a line of weather walks the length of India in 38 days. Follow the front.*

**Chapters** (a calendar scrubber pinned to the side; the date advances with scroll):
1. **20–31 May: the sea.** Somali jet, Andaman 22 May, a cloud bank massing over the Arabian Sea. Data-viz of winds as flowing strokes.
2. **1 June: the gate (Kerala).** The onset declaration, coconut palms bending, a whole-screen downpour. "Rain advances like a king" (Ritusamhara).
3. **5–15 June: the climb.** The Western Ghats, the NE, Mumbai (11 June), Bengal. Vignettes: a sea wall, umbrellas, a shared umbrella in a film homage (no lyrics).
4. **15–20 June: the plains.** The NLM line pulses north in surges. Villages: paper boats, frogs, ploughing, peacocks. Kalidasa's Yaksha peak gets its rain (~16 June).
5. **27 June–8 July: the dry west.** Delhi 27 June, then the desert holding out (Bikaner ~6 July). A single cloud over sand. The country is covered on 8 July.
6. **July–August: the deep monsoon and the cloudburst.** Himalaya, 100 mm/hour, a respectful pause.
7. **17 Sept → 15 Oct: the withdrawal.** The line walks back. Clearing, autumn light. Closing data: 70–90% of India's annual rain falls June to September (IMD report).

**Art direction:** **"Weather-office atlas meets miniature."**
- An antique IMD isochrone map engraved on aged paper. The front is drawn as a living gold line, and the sky above the map is painted in miniature style.
- Each isochrone opens a circular **vignette window**, like Kangra's oval, into a moment of life.

**Signature interaction:**
- **The date scrubber.** Scrolling is time, and the front sweeps the map. Hovering a city shows its normal onset day.
- A toggle compares **this year vs normal** (e.g. 2025's early sweep by 29 June).

**Sound:** the drone's pitch and texture shift region by region. The coast has bigger waves of noise; the desert is sparse with a single frog. Malhar enters as the front reaches each region. Thunder is spatialised along the front line.

**Location note:** only use the public IMD cities from the table above. Never use the creator's own towns.

### Which one I'd pick, and why: **Concept A, *The Cloud Messenger*.**
1. **It has a story engine, not just a mood.** A sender, a messenger, a route, a recipient and a delivered message give every scroll a purpose and the ending a real emotional payoff. B is a lovely lexicon but plotless. C is excellent data storytelling but cooler emotionally.
2. **Its literary depth is real and unique.** Kalidasa's poem is the source text of the Indian rain imagination: a "new genre", per Ryder. Award juries reward pieces that only one culture could have made. Ryder's 1912 public-domain verses give quotable, authoritative copy at no licence risk.
3. **It absorbs the best of B and C.** The rain words become chapter titles and the intensity ladder (B), and the IMD onset isochrones become the map the cloud flies over (C). The ~16 June date for the Ramagiri region links the myth to the measured monsoon.
4. **The art direction is coherent and ownable.** Live Kangra or Malwa miniature (gold lightning, crane rows, scalloped skies, floral frames) is rarely done well on the web, and it avoids the generic rainy-window look.
5. **The interaction is meaningful and not a game.** "You are the cloud; what you carry becomes the storm" is a reading and scrolling mechanic with consequences. There is no score and no fail state.
6. **It is distinct from earlier Barsaat directions** (the fog-window and paper-boat versions), and safe on privacy: entirely classical geography, no personal town.

**Risks to manage:**
- The first half of the poem is full of names Western readers won't know. Keep each stop to one image and one line.
- Keep the erotic verses out. Ryder's choices are already mild.
- The cloudburst must stay respectful.
- Sound must start on a gesture.
- The user's message stays on-device.
